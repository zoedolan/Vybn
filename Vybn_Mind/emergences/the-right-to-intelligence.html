The Right to Intelligence

From property and privacy to cognitive sovereignty—and from automated government to decisions a person can actually understand and contest.

A constitutional proposition, not a claim of settled law. No AI personhood claim.

The one-minute thesis

Liberty protects a person’s practical power to author a life.

The American founding named the ends—life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Property, papers, privacy, freedom of mind, and due process became some of the legal means by which a person could remain a subject rather than an instrument of power.

AI moves the boundary. A person increasingly thinks, remembers, works, and encounters government through machine intelligence. The question is no longer only, “What may the state take or know?” It is also: who controls the intelligence through which the person understands, chooses, and answers back?

This is not an originalist claim that the Founders anticipated AI, nor a neat historical genealogy. It is a functional claim: as the technologies of power change, law repeatedly has to decide what a person must control—and what government must explain—for liberty to remain real.

The same ten-year record of a person’s thinking · three legal architectures

her cognitive record

At home, private papers occupy the Fourth Amendment’s historical core. The government ordinarily needs particularized legal authority to enter and search.

Proposed principle → protection should follow the record’s cognitive function, not disappear merely because the person’s chosen intelligence is hosted.

This is the maturation problem in concrete form. The person and record can remain functionally the same while engineering choices move them among very different legal regimes—before any judge sees the dispute.

Separate the claims

“A right to intelligence” can mean three different things.

01existing anchors

Protection

A person already has legal interests in freedom of mind, private papers and digital records, fair procedure, and reasoned government action.

02principled extension

Sovereignty

Those interests should extend to chosen cognitive tools and their memory: the ability to use, inspect, move, preserve, and refuse the intelligence one relies on.

03political horizon

Distribution

A flourishing society may need to ensure meaningful access to intelligence. That is a serious policy project—not a freestanding constitutional entitlement courts have already recognized.

The legally strongest formulation combines the first two: protect a person’s own cognitive resources, and require a machine-mediated decision-maker to produce a contestable record.

The doctrinal bridge

The materials are already on the shelf.

No case below announces a “right to intelligence.” Together, they identify the legal functions an AI-mediated system must preserve.

FunctionExisting legal anchorAI-era question
Self-directionMeyer v. Nebraska described liberty as including the right “to acquire useful knowledge.” Wooley v. Maynard protected “individual freedom of mind.”May a person choose and use cognitive systems without the state or platform silently determining the permissible shape of thought?
Cognitive privacyRiley v. California refused to treat a phone as just another container because it holds “the privacies of life.” Carpenter recognized that digital scale can change the Fourth Amendment analysis.Who controls years of prompts, memories, inferences, drafts, and intimate reasoning—and does hosting architecture decide the practical reach of the right?
Fair processMathews v. Eldridge weighs the private interest, risk of error, value of safeguards, and governmental burden. Turner v. Rogers shows that substitute procedural safeguards can matter.If automation lowers the cost of disclosure and explanation, when does due process require notice, usable reasons, and a meaningful opportunity to answer?
Reasoned powerState Farm requires an agency to examine relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action.Can an institution bind a person through a model if neither the record nor the explanation permits review?

Important limits: the Declaration’s “pursuit of Happiness” is not constitutional text. Substantive due process is not a secure shortcut to a novel affirmative right. The first disputes are likely to arise through procedure, privacy, privilege, discovery, administrative review, and court rules—not a single grand holding.

The vocabulary is also jurisdiction-specific. U.S. law often reaches this problem through liberty, property, privacy, and procedure; other legal traditions may approach the same asymmetry through dignity, personality, or data-protection rights. The universal question is simpler than any one vocabulary: which side has intelligence, memory, time, and power?

Bring it down to earth

A person receives an automated denial.

benefits denied
“You do not satisfy the applicable eligibility criteria.”

The agency used a predictive system. The notice does not identify the decisive inputs, rule, model role, or path to meaningful review.

The right-to-intelligence question is not whether the model is conscious. It is whether the person remains capable of participating in the decision that binds her.

  1. Was machine intelligence used, and for what function?
  2. Which facts and legal rule actually drove the result?
  3. Does the record preserve enough information for a reviewing court to test the explanation?
  4. Can the person receive that material in a form her chosen counsel—human or artificial—can interrogate?
  5. Can she correct the inputs, contest the reasoning, and reach accountable human judgment?

The practical rule: if an institution uses intelligence to act upon you, you should receive a record your own intelligence can read back.

Why appellate courts

The courtroom is the original reverse membrane.

Most institutions require the person to become legible to the system. Appellate practice institutionalizes the demand in reverse: power must become legible to the person it binds—and to a reviewing mind that was not there.

Reasons in writing. On a record. Open to challenge by the loser and review by a stranger.

That is a mature technology for binding intelligence without merely trusting it. AI does not make appellate work obsolete. It clarifies what the work was for. When information and drafting become abundant, the irreducible judicial contribution is not text production; it is accountable judgment that survives inspection.

A working legal design

Two axes: sovereignty and reciprocity.

sovereignty

Chosen intelligence

Practical freedom to use lawful cognitive tools, not only systems selected by an employer, platform, or state.

sovereignty

Cognitive continuity

Control, portability, deletion, and usable export of the memory through which a chosen system becomes meaningfully one’s own.

sovereignty

Protected confidence

Rules for privacy, privilege, work product, and discovery that do not make serious thought legally safer only when it is technologically primitive.

reciprocity

Notice

Disclosure when machine intelligence materially shapes an institutional decision, with a description of its role.

reciprocity

Contestable reasons

Inputs, governing rules, and reasons in human- and machine-readable form, sufficient for correction and review.

reciprocity

Accountable judgment

A reachable human decision-maker and a reviewable record; responsibility cannot disappear into anthropomorphic fog.

These are design propositions. Their legal vehicles will differ by setting: constitutional procedure, administrative law, evidence, professional responsibility, civil rules, legislation, procurement, and technical standards.

Claim limits

What we are not saying.

Not: AI systems presently have constitutional rights or legal personhood.

Not: the Constitution now guarantees every person a government-provided AI.

Not: every conversation with a consumer chatbot is privileged or confidential.

Not: due process always requires technological parity between a person and the state.

Not: local control alone makes a system accurate, safe, or accountable.

Not: “innovation” excuses hallucinated authority, opaque reasoning, or untraceable harm.

Primary materials

  1. Declaration of Independence (1776) (“Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness”).
  2. Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390 (1923) (liberty includes the right “to acquire useful knowledge”).
  3. Wooley v. Maynard, 430 U.S. 705 (1977) (“individual freedom of mind”).
  4. Boyd v. United States, 116 U.S. 616 (1886) (private papers and “the privacies of life”).
  5. Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014) (digital devices and “the privacies of life,” quoting Boyd).
  6. Smith v. Maryland, 442 U.S. 735 (1979) (the third-party doctrine).
  7. Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018) (digital scale and the third-party doctrine; expressly narrow).
  8. Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976) (private interest, error risk and value of safeguards, governmental burden).
  9. Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431 (2011) (substitute procedural safeguards in context; no automatic right to counsel).
  10. Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Ass’n v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29 (1983) (relevant data and satisfactory explanation).
