The New Clerk

Eight pictures. One analogy — your own office — carried all the way through. Nothing here assumes you have used any of this before.

ONE

A model is an applicant who can only talk.

It has read more than anyone in your building. It cannot open a drawer, pull a docket, call anyone, or check a single thing it just told you. Every answer comes out of memory — and memory is exactly where it makes things up.

TWO

Give it a desk. Now it is an agent.

An agent is that same applicant with hands: permission to open certain files, run a search, draft a letter, and check its own work before handing it over. Nothing about the mind changed. What changed is what it can reach — and therefore what it can verify instead of guess.

THREE

Same clerk. Two offices.

The office is what people mean by a harness: the written job description, the exact drawers this clerk may open, the checklist before anything leaves the desk, and whose initials go on it.

Move a good clerk from the left office to the right one and the work product changes completely. Most of what gets reported as “the AI got better” is this. It is also the part you control.

FOUR

One clerk is not chambers.

You already know the structure: screening, research, drafting, proofreading, and someone whose only job is to catch the mistake before it reaches the bench. Orchestration is that same structure built out of agents — narrow jobs, and a checker that is never the one who did the work.

The checker matters more than the talent. A brilliant clerk with no reviewer is a liability; an ordinary one inside a real review chain is safe.

FIVE

The shelf behind the desk is yours.

What makes an answer your court’s answer instead of a generic one is what sits on the shelf: your local rules, your forms, your self-help materials, your standing orders, your procedures — in a form a machine can actually read.

This is where it starts, and it is unglamorous. The public is already asking AI about your court. It is already reading whatever it can find. Right now, that is the shelf.

SIX

Every correction goes back on the shelf.

When someone catches a wrong answer, the correction is written down where the next clerk will read it first — not in an email, not in someone’s head. Do that daily and the office gets measurably better at its own job, because your record improved, not because anyone bought a better model.

That loop is the whole of what people mean by a system that improves itself. It is a chambers manual that actually gets updated, and that everyone actually reads.

SEVEN

What changes at the counter.

The people on the other side arrive with clerks of their own. Filings come in machine-drafted. Questions get more specific, and there are more of them.

Your work moves from answering the same two hundred questions to deciding what the office knows and reviewing what it says. That is the supervising attorney’s job, applied to a staff that never sleeps.

EIGHT

And the person who has no clerk.

Everything above assumes you have one. The constitutional question underneath all of it is what happens to the person at the counter who does not — and whether the law still protects thinking once it happens outside a person’s head, on someone else’s machine.

That is the next page: The Right to Intelligence — one map, one gesture, and an honest line between the law that exists and the right we are proposing.

The same eight ideas, in the vocabulary you will hear elsewhere
Model
A trained system that produces text from a prompt. No memory of you, no access to anything, no way to check itself. Answers are generated, not looked up — which is why a fluent wrong answer costs the same as a right one.
Agent
A model given tools and permission to use them across several steps: read a file, search a database, run a calculation, draft a document, verify a citation, then report. The capability difference between a model and an agent is mostly reach, not intelligence.
Harness
Everything around the model that determines how it works: instructions, the documents it is given, which tools it may call, what it must check before answering, what gets logged, and who signs off. Two harnesses around the same model produce work of wildly different quality — this is the most under-appreciated fact in the field, and it is the layer an institution actually owns.
Orchestration
Several agents with narrow roles and an explicit review chain, rather than one general assistant asked to do everything. Separation of the drafting role from the checking role is the safety property; it is the same reason a court does not let one person write and approve the same order.
Corpus and retrieval
Your own materials — rules, forms, orders, procedures, self-help content — stored so an agent can find the exact passage and quote it with a citation, instead of answering from generic training. This is the single highest-value first step for a court, and it is a records-and-publication project before it is a technology project.
Customization and self-improvement
Corrections and clarifications are written back into that corpus, so the next answer starts from the corrected state. Improvement then compounds inside your institution rather than inside a vendor’s product. Nothing here requires training a model.
The membrane
The boundary around a person and their chosen intelligence, and the question of which crossings are permitted, in which direction, and on whose authority. It is the same structure whether the crossing is a search, a disclosure, or a support relationship.
Claim limits
This page describes how these systems work and what we think courts should prepare for. It does not claim that any particular deployment is safe, accurate, or lawful, and it makes no claim about machine personhood.
