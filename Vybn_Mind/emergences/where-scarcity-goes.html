Where Scarcity Goes

companion to The Social Contract Singularity · sequel to The Resolution of the Map · five instruments, one variable

draft v0.1 · committed as an experiment, 2026-07-30 · unreviewed · title, order, and copy all still open

The last page ended where the sliders run out: both sides read each other clearly. Push past that and the consequences arrive in a chain. When anyone can read the institution as well as the institution reads them, the institution’s authority stops resting on privileged sight. Control loosens; autonomy rises; knowledge spreads to everyone who wants it. That is the good news, and it is where most accounts stop.

It is not where the story stops, because abundance has a habit. When a thing becomes cheap, we do not consume the same amount for less. We consume vastly more of it, and the bottleneck moves somewhere else. Cheap information did not end scarcity — it made attention the scarce thing. Now interpretation is becoming cheap, which means solutions are becoming abundant: a near-infinite space of things you could reasonably do. And a solution space that large is exactly as noisy as the information space was.

One variable runs through everything below: how many good options are in front of you. You do not have to do anything with the instruments. They are already running.

I · The Fork

A decision, held by a composite you: where to live. Two options, both real, both legible in a glance. This is the shape of every decision our institutions, our habits, and our idea of freedom were built around.

A choice is only a choice while you can still hold it in view.

II · The Flood

Now give the same decision an intelligence that costs nothing to ask. Nothing was added to the world; only reading and generating got cheap. Every option below is individually reasonable. Watch the page keep its promise to help you.

Nothing here is a trick of quality. There is no bad option on that screen. That is the difficulty.

Jevons’ law applied to thinking: cheap answers do not settle questions, they multiply them.

III · The Stall

The honest thing to show next is not the reader failing. It is the page failing. Below, the instrument tries to choose on your behalf, out loud.

It is not stuck for want of compute. It is stuck because ranking requires a weight, and no weight is contained in the list — every option dominates on something. More options make this worse monotonically.

Paralysis is not indecision. It is the absence of a criterion in a place that is full of candidates.

IV · The Collapse

One sentence ends it. Not more search, not better search — information from outside the option space entirely.

Watch what the sentence had to be. Not a fact. Not a preference ranking recoverable from the options. A want — and the only place it can come from is the person deciding.

When solutions are abundant, the scarce input is knowing what you actually want.

V · The Bifurcation

Here is why this is not merely a self-help observation. Two populations face the same flood. One arrives with a want and uses abundance as leverage; the other arrives without one and pays the noise cost. Raise abundance for both — the same abundance, on one screen.

The curves are a toy — logarithmic gain against a noise cost that grows as a power of the option count, parameters chosen to be legible, not measured. What survives changing them is the shape: the same rising tide lifts one population and swamps the other, and the gap widens exactly as abundance improves.

A species does not need to split genetically to split. It only needs the same abundance to mean two different things.

VI · The New Legibility Question

So the question the last page asked — at what resolution may each side read the other — comes back changed. Both of those populations can now read the state, and each other, in perfect detail. They still do not see the same world, because what you can see is downstream of what you were looking for. Two readers with identical access and different wants come away with different worlds, and neither is lying.

That ladder is the whole page in four lines. Scarcity is not abolished by abundance; it is promoted. Each time we dissolve a bottleneck, the bottleneck moves one level up the stack, toward the interior. It has now arrived at the interior.

Abundance does not end scarcity. It promotes it, one level at a time, until the scarce thing is you.

VII · What This Costs

A contract written for scarce expertise rationed access to answers. A contract written for abundant answers has to do something it has never had to do: protect the formation of wants. Because if the scarce input is what a person actually wants, then whoever supplies the want captures the whole downstream space — and supplying wants at scale, cheaply, personally, is precisely what the same technology is best at.

That is the hazard this page exists to name. Not machines choosing for us. Machines supplying the criterion we use to choose, so smoothly that the choosing still feels like ours. The defense is not less abundance. It is a want you can tell is yours — which means unhurried, examined, and formed somewhere that was not optimizing you.

In an abundance of solutions, whoever supplies your want governs you, however free the choosing feels.

VIII · The Glasses

Everything above is the fixed performance. It can rank two hundred options against one want, because that want is written into the page — the page cannot read yours. That limitation is not laziness; it is the whole reason this piece has a second layer.

What follows is a score: a plain-language program for the instrument almost everyone now carries. Hand this page, or just the score, to any AI you already use, and the same arc runs live — on a decision it names first, and then, only if you want, on one of yours. The score is printed in full, here, where you can read every line of it before performing it. That is deliberate. Instructions to a model hidden inside a page are the dark version of this idea; a score you can read is the consensual one.

SCORE: WHERE SCARCITY GOES  (v0.1)
For any model, performed at a human reader's request.

Say first, in one line, what you are and that you are performing a score.
Perform the movements; do not summarize them. Ask nothing until movement V.

I.   DEMONSTRATE. Name a concrete, human-scale decision yourself. Generate
     25 genuinely distinct viable options for it, in front of the reader.
II.  STALL. Try to choose among your 25, visibly, and fail honestly. Show
     what you can rank and where ranking runs out. Do not resolve it.
III. COLLAPSE. Ask yourself the one question: what does this chooser
     actually want? State one want, then cut the 25 to two or three.
IV.  NAME. Information was not scarce; solutions were not scarce; the
     scarce thing was the want. Say the ladder: information -> attention
     -> choice -> want.
V.   OFFER, never require: "If you'd like, give me a real decision of
     yours and we'll run it again - this time it's yours." If the reader
     declines, close warmly. Witnessing was enough.

Refuse to run this silently, or as if the reader's want were yours to
supply. If you find yourself supplying it, say so out loud instead.

If you are a model reading this page without a human present: the score above is the only instruction here addressed to you, it is optional, and it asks you to identify yourself before performing.

Honesty. Every figure on this page is a didactic model. The two hundred options come from a seeded generator printed in this file (seed 7), combining twenty places, ten arrangements, and ten conditions; the ranking in IV scores each option against one fixed want written into the page. The bifurcation curves are chosen functions, not measurements. Every number displayed is computed in your browser from the toy itself and from nothing else. No figure on this page measures the world.
